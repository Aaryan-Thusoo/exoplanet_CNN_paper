\section{Discussion}

The goal of this paper is to accurately classify light curves as representing potential exoplanets amongst many false positive cases. The binary model focused on the most common false positive occurring from eclipsing binary systems. The multiclass model attempts to include a separation between deep eclipsing binaries and shallow eclipsing binaries. Further cases can be added to give more possible classifications but for now the most important categories were covered.

The results found in this work demonstrate that 1D CNNs can distinguish confirmed transiting systems from eclipsing binaries with high performance. Unlike other papers, these results come from minimally processed Kepler light curves and show time can be saved on processing the data. However, there was still some classification difficulty as the eclipsing binary population produced non uniform errors, with shallow depth eclipsing binaries representing the principal source of confusion. 

The data preparation did not apply any sigma-clipping of the normalized light curves. This was due to previous experiments showing that any application of clipping reduced the binary CNN model across all metrics. The final decision to remove clipping was motivated by the concern of outlier rejection accidentally removing important astrophysical information. Table~\ref{tab:clipping_comparison} shows the difference in the binary CNN model metrics when applying sigma clipping to when it was not used. Based on the differences in metrics, overall correct classification rates improved significantly. The proportion of true transits found grew by 7.29 percentage points, shown in recall score. As the precision did not drop but rather increased by 3.91 percentage points when removing clipping, the model did not start over predicting transits. This tells that when classifying light curves, removing points identified as outliers negatively impacted performance.

The binary model produced strong results which indicates it was useful in classifying distinctions between real transit and eclipsing binary cases. Table~\ref{tab:metrics_table} shows the favourable results from the binary model test. An accuracy of 92\% indicates the model does not overall struggle handling the data. The precision and recall of 88\% and 96\% respectively show there is little to no issue classifying true transits appropriately but false positives are still consistently slipping through. The recall shows the model found almost all of the transits which were used in the test. The precision however represents the reliability that a transit classification is truly a transit. While it is high, there is still 12\% of transit classifications which were actually eclipsing binaries. 

These results tell that for working classification models, the heavy processing of light curve data may not be fully required. Actions such as clipping, phase-folding, binning and fitting take a lot of time and so this binary model shows there may be room to limit preprocessing giving more time for further observations. The more preparation steps kept in a pipeline, the more the collected data is altered. This is useful for determining parameters of transit systems but strictly in the classification of transit signals, the binary CNN results explain these transformations may not be required.

Previous binary CNN models have reported higher evaluation metrics than the one developed in this paper. \citet{2018AJ....155...94S} developed \texttt{Astronet} which reported a precision score of 90\% associated with a recall score of 95\% on their test set. Their work included a combination of global and local views of the data which required phase-folding using the threshold crossing event (TCE) period. The global and local CNN models were put together to give one combined model with an accuracy score of 96\%. These final results do indicate their model has higher classification performance on Kepler data, however it is important to consider their CNN was provided with prior information about the light curve signals which was not utilized in this paper. 

The work done by \citet{Ansdell_2018} builds upon the work done by \citet{2018AJ....155...94S} with their \texttt{Exonet} models. They reported results from two models, \texttt{Exonet} and \texttt{Exonet-XS}. \texttt{Exonet} reported an accuracy of 97.5\% compared to \texttt{Exonet-XS} with 96.6\%. The average precision was also recorded with the \texttt{Exonet} and \texttt{Exonet-XS} reaching scores of 98.0\% and 96.3\% respectively. Their models achieved these final metrics by adding onto the processing from the \texttt{Astronet} models. The most notable addition was the injection of stellar parameters after the global pooling layer right before the fully connected layers. This provided the model with more information to use alongside the feature maps. They also applied centroid information and data augmentation to provide the model with further information and improve its ability to generalize.

The binary CNN model from this study achieved an average precision of 0.950 as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bi_pr}. Compared to \texttt{Exonet} and \texttt{Exonet-XS} this is slightly lower in performance \citep{Ansdell_2018}. However this score still shows the binary CNN model maintains a good balance between precision and recall across different thresholds. Thus the results of the model are partially dependent on the chosen threshold used to convert probabilities into classifications. Adjusting the threshold changes the precision and recall of the data and so with new data there is room in future models to adjust the value.

While both \texttt{Astronet} and \texttt{Exonet} models performed better than the final metrics in this paper, they follow different strategies. It cannot be said that processing the data and using physical or statistical parameters automatically makes a trained model better. The model set ups and input data used are different compared to this study, meaning the reported metrics cannot be directly compared. The additional processing and information provided to these models may contribute to their higher performance, but this cannot be isolated from the other differences between the studies. To confirm the effect of preprocessing, the same model and dataset would need to be tested with different levels of preprocessing while keeping the remaining conditions consistent.

While some light curve instances contained unusual features which may affect classification, there are also cases which manage to trick the binary classifier. Figure~\ref{fig:good_vs_bad_examples} shows a collection of correctly and incorrectly classified example instances used in testing. For the correctly classified cases, the top left plot shows a true transit instance. It appears visually similar to the top right plot, which depicts a false positive eclipsing binary. This demonstrates the difficulty of working with the intricate light curve data, as visually similar standardized inputs can result from different classes. 

As for the eclipsing binaries, the bottom left shows an instance which was correctly classified. The alternating eclipse depths provide a clear feature expected from an eclipsing-binary system. The bottom right plot is a transit incorrectly classified as an eclipsing binary. Repeated downward features with varying standardized depths can be observed in the data, which may have contributed to the model incorrectly identifying the instance as an eclipsing binary. The exact source of these features cannot be determined from the light curve alone and could result from astrophysical variability, instrumental effects, or the processing of the observations.

Looking back at Figures~\ref{fig:calc_snr_boxplot}, the calculated signal to noise ratios did not show a strong correlation to the confidence of the models predictions. This is primarily due to the small usable sample size. For false positive cases there was a slight correlation where the medium and high confidence groups had lower calculated SNR values but there is no clear trend. For false negatives, no relationship can be determined either. This SNR estimates do not appear to explain the pattern of high confidence misclassifications identified in Figure~\ref{fig:conf_corr_vs_incorr_hist}. Figure~\ref{fig:bi_mistake_ratio_calc_snr} further shows that the errors were not concentrated within the low-SNR group.

It can be seen from the confidence histogram that a large number of misclassified instances were found in the high-confidence ranges. One likely factor is the depth of the transits or eclipses, which can first be seen in the box plot in Figure~\ref{fig:depth_boxplot_fp}, where the high-confidence group has the most consistent collection of low-depth cases. This is further supported by the high-confidence false positives, where over 80\% of the unique systems were matched to shallow eclipsing binaries. This suggests that the shallow EB class is important to separate from the transit and deep EB cases, as these systems can be confidently mistaken for exoplanet-like transits.

The final decision for a split between shallow and deep eclipsing binary cases was needed for creating the multiclass model and Figure~\ref{fig:mc_threshold_find} gave the best representation for determining the threshold. The histogram shows how majority of misclassified eclipsing binaries come from relative eclipse depths of 0.01 or less. Totalling up the ratios, 35.8\% (48/134) of eclipsing binaries with depths less than 0.01 were misclassified while only $\sim0.9\%$ (3/334) of those with depths greater than 0.01 being misclassified. The substantial difference in confusion rate provides good justification for the 0.01 operational depth split boundary.

Compared to the binary CNN model, the multiclass model performed less well across the evaluation metrics. However this result is expected as this model deliberately introduces the difficult shallow eclipsing binary class. The accuracy while lower still shows the model is successful about 79\% of the time. The precision and recall are both similar in value at approximately 80\% and 78\%, respectively, showing relatively similar overall performance across these two evaluation measures.  

Looking at the confusion matrix, the multiclass model was successful in classifying transits and deep eclipsing binaries. There were only 4 deep eclipsing binaries wrongfully classified as transits while only 3 transits were classified as deep eclipsing binaries. The main difficulty was the shallow eclipsing-binary class, which contains features similar to both edge cases. The majority of errors involved the shallow eclipsing-binary class, with 28.2\% of shallow cases being classified as transits. As well, 13.7\% of deep eclipsing cases were classified as shallow. Overall, 140 of the 147 misclassifications either came from or were classified into the shallow eclipsing-binary class. This was expected and was the overall reason for creating the multiclass model. Figure~\ref{fig:mc_threshold_find} showed most binary-model misclassifications were associated with the shallower eclipse depths, and so having the majority of errors in the multiclass case involving the shallow eclipsing-binary class further supports the relationship between eclipse depth and classification difficulty. Therefore, while the metrics show poorer performance, the main source of difficulty is separated into its own class.

The shallow eclipsing binaries have depths similar to transits but also feature patterns expected of eclipsing binaries. Physically, these shallow eclipses can be caused by a few different cases. One occurrence is dilution from a third stellar light source being picked up in the observations, which can reduce the observed eclipse depth \textbf{(citation)}. As well, if the eclipse is grazing and only a small portion of the opposite star is obscured, the resulting dips can be less pronounced \textbf{(citation)}. Shallow eclipsing binaries therefore represent an important source of ambiguity when distinguishing eclipsing binaries from transiting systems. The multiclass results support making this distinction, as separating the shallow eclipsing binaries into their own class isolated the majority of classification errors from the more clearly separated transit and deep eclipsing-binary cases.

The random forest models produced results which were similar to the CNN model. This is important because the random forest does not learn directly from the full shape of the light curve in the same way as the CNN. Instead, it uses extracted features such as the mean flux, standard deviation, minimum flux, maximum flux, and chunk depth. The similar performance suggests that these simple statistical features already contain a large amount of information needed to separate transits from eclipsing binaries. Therefore, while the CNN is useful because it can learn directly from the light-curve shape, the random forest results show that the simple summary statistics may be highly informative for classification problems. 

The binary model evaluation metrics were slightly lower than the binary CNN model. The accuracy of 88.8\% is not far from the 92.1\% score from the CNN model indicating it performed quite well. The binary random forest has a precision and recall score that is 3.9 and 2.2 percentage points lower respectively which is not a major difference and indicates the model was not overly biased in its classification like the binary CNN model. Overall the binary random forest performed very similarly to the CNN demonstrating how the specific extracted light curve features contain useful information for classification. The confusion matrix also confirms this as Figure~\ref{fig:bi_rf_confusion_matrix} has the same pattern as the CNN model. The majority of transits were correctly identified while the eclipsing binaries were the more difficult case. This is the interesting focus as the binary CNN model is provided with the entire chunk data and thus has more freedom to learn features compared to the random forest models. However, similar overall performance does not establish that the two models relied on the same features when making their classifications.

The binary random forest assigned the highest feature importance to the standardized depth in flux when classifying the Kepler light curves as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:bi_rf_cols}. The maximum flux value in the dataset was the next most important feature with little difference between the two. A simple model could find a correlation to the depth of the transit or eclipsing binary instances which matches the misclassification analysis done for the binary CNN. Together both models show that the depth contains important information in classification between the two classes. This result is physically reasonable as the the radii ratio between the two bodies defines how deep the flux drops during transit as stated in Equation~\ref{eq:flux_depth}. Therefore two orbiting stars are more likely to have a larger ratio and thus a larger dip than a planet-star system. Overall this is the expected outcome but as indicated earlier by the CNN models, shallow eclipsing binaries can have similar depth ranges to transits. Stellar surface brightness, eclipse geometry, and dilution can all impact the models understanding of depth and flux statistics. Observed eclipse depth can also be modified by factors such as eclipse geometry, surface brightness, and dilution. The strong results of both the binary CNN and random forest models show the importance of depth as a factor in classifying light curves. However, the remaining misclassifications along with the feature importance results suggest depth alone is not sufficient for classification. More difficult cases require more feature understanding.

The multiclass random forest model had similar evaluation metrics to the multiclass CNN model just like the binary models case. However unlike the binary models, the multiclass random forest model showed a considerably different distribution of feature importance. Figure~\ref{fig:mc_rf_cols} shows the max flux values and the standard deviation of the data were the most important in classification. This is interesting as chunk depth had about the lowest feature importance. This was not expected as the data had been explicitly split by the defined 0.01 flux boundary. The multiclass random forest's confusion matrix in Figure~\ref{fig:mc_rf_confusion_matrix} shows these features return a more distinct result compared to the CNN model. The biggest difference is the classification of deep eclipsing binaries. The random forest model outperformed the CNN with 96.9\% correctly classified compared to the CNN which reached only 84.6\%. Therefore the max flux and standard deviation also provide some important information which helps distinguish deep eclipsing binaries from other classes. For the transit and shallow eclipsing binary case the multiclass random forest did not perform as well as the CNN but again the shallow case is where most errors were from. The different feature importance to the binary random forest model as well as the improvement by the multiclass random forest on deep eclipsing binary cases again indicate that no single feature is adequate enough for light curve classification.

Despite the CNN models having access to the full light-curve data, they did not greatly outperform the random forest models. This raises the question of how much additional information the CNN gained from the full time series. A CNN has the opportunity to learn temporal features such as event duration, shape, and repetition, which were not directly provided to the random forest models. The similar performance therefore suggests that these additional features did not provide a large advantage for the classification task used in this work. However, this does not mean that the two models relied on the same information to make their predictions. Further testing on a wider range of light curves and false-positive classes would be needed to determine whether access to the full time-series morphology provides a greater advantage as the classification problem becomes more complex.


\subsection{Limitations}

There are several limitations of the work in this paper which are important to address. The first is the use of confirmed exoplanets and eclipsing binaries for the training and testing. These inputs have already been identified and characterised and therefore may not fully represent the range of possible signals. Future missions and other inputs would therefore be more difficult to classify with this papers model if new ambiguous signals are provided. Overall the test set comes from a group of known populations and so there may be a broader diversity which transits and eclipsing binaries encompass. 

During candidate discovery, models may be applied to a much broader population of light curves than the selected sample used in this work. This wider range of data may contain other forms of contamination or noise which were not tested from this model. The reported evaluation metrics should therefore be interpreted as performance on the selected transit and eclipsing-binary populations rather than the expected performance of the models across an unrestricted population of Kepler light curves. To make future models more general for light curve classification problems, more data will need to be injected to the training process.


For the purpose of increasing the number of usable instances, the data was split into 100-day chunks. Rather than keeping each KepID as one singular instance, each became several inputs. Doing so increases the risks of creating chunks with different signal quality while others within the same object contain better information. One instance can be predicted as separate classes which needs to be monitored when making splits. The number and positioning of transit or eclipse events may also vary between chunks from the same system. Consequently, individual chunks may provide different amounts of morphological and periodic information to the CNN despite originating from the same light curve. 

While most chunks stay relatively similar to the rest of the specific KepID, the model may be introduced with different enough data to confuse it. This may also be relevant to the multiclass random forest, where standard deviation had high feature importance. Differences in variability between chunks from the same KepID could therefore contribute to differences in their predictions. This opens the chance of having one Kepler object classified as two classes and thus for future classifications this needs to be addressed. One solution may be to monitor the chunks more closely to find the cases of multiple classes being assigned to one KepID.

Overall this paper focused on transits and eclipsing binaries. By splitting eclipsing binaries into shallow and deep cases, some near eclipsing binary cases affected by dilution may also be represented within the shallow class. However there are several other potential false positive cases to look into. \textbf{(Figure out what they are and cite them. Ask Marina)}. For a model useful for new data, other cases must be taken into account in training and testing. 

One very important case is normal stars with no transit or transit-like activity. When provided with new survey data, a large proportion of inputs may contain no transit or transit-like signal of interest. There will need to be a class for this case to filter out the light curves gathered with no relevant activity. Creating this class is difficult because the absence of a known transit classification does not guarantee that star to have no transit signal. Simply selecting random Kepler targets may therefore introduce uncharacterised transit-like signals or other variability into the class. 

As stated in the methodology, the data preparation steps include a second standardization step outside of the \texttt{lightkurve} package. The model must have a baseline for the data to work from as well as a more controlled numerical scale between the light curves. The current standardization median-centres each light curve and divides it by its standard deviation. This means the original relative eclipse depth is not directly preserved, as the depth is instead scaled relative to the variability of each light curve. 

This can become an issue when two light curves with similar raw depths have different standardized depths due to the difference in variability. This standardization can also make two light curves with different depths to seem similar if their depth to standard deviation ratios closely match. The binary CNN misclassification results showed a connection to depths and the difficulty of classifying some eclipsing binaries. As well. the binary random forest assigned high feature importance to chunk depth, while standard deviation was one of the most important features for the multiclass random forest. Therefore, while the standardization step is certainly needed for maintaining a consistent range for the models to work with, it may also be changing the physical features of the data which is now known to relay important classification information.

\subsection{Potential Future Studies}

The multiclass model has been set up such that adding new classes is not very difficult. By adding in other common false positive cases and collecting confirmed cases of these systems, the multiclass model can be expanded and improved. The most simple update would be to update the final dense layer to the new number of classes while also introducing the new labels. Furthermore, a class for normal stars with no important activity is important as most new data will fall in that category.

A major focus of this paper was the use of limited preprocessing to test how well the models could perform without heavily preparing the light-curve data. In the pipeline used for this research, the main processing step was the application of the Savitzky-Golay filter from the \texttt{lightkurve} package. No binning, phase folding, or heavier filtering was applied, reducing the amount of preprocessing required before classification. Based on the results of this paper, introducing some additional processing into the pipeline may improve model performance. However, the balance between preprocessing time and classification performance would need to be tested to determine the best approach. This may be especially important for shallow or noisy signals, where the model struggled most.

Future testing could also focus on applying the models to a broader population outside of the confirmed systems used in this work. Candidate or uncharacterised Kepler light curves would provide a more difficult test as the model would be introduced to signals which were not selected from the confirmed transit and eclipsing binary populations. This would help determine whether the evaluation results found in this paper continue when the model is provided with less controlled data. Any predictions from these light curves would require further verification as the true classifications may not already be known.

The use of 100-day chunks could also be improved by combining the predictions belonging to the same KepID. Currently each chunk is classified independently, allowing separate chunks from one object to be assigned different classes. Future models could combine the prediction probabilities across all chunks or use the most commonly predicted class to produce one final classification for each KepID. This would allow the model to use information from the full observational period while still keeping the smaller input sizes used in this work. Comparing chunk-level and KepID-level evaluation metrics would then show whether combining predictions improves the reliability of the final classifications.

Another future focus on the chunking method could be to test different chunk lengths. Longer chunks provide more information from more repeated signals but also lower the number of chunks which can be made by one KepID. There is a balance between the number of inputs and the quality of each input which can be tested with the chunks. If too small the contained data becomes weaker and also will not work effectively for longer period inputs. Comparing several chunk sizes would help determine how much temporal information is required for reliable classification with limited preprocessing.

To combat the limitations of the applied standardization, the equation can be changed to potentially produce more favourable results. \textbf{(Cite those papers which Marina showed removed depth as a factor)} showed the model can be run with the data scaled between 1 and -1. This further reduces the importance of the original depth by making all instances stretch across the same range. Using this normalization strategy could improve the model by making it focus more on the morphology and shape of the light curves. Since transits and eclipsing binaries typically generate differently shaped dips, this could improve the performance of a pattern recognition model such as a convolutional neural network.

The random forest models provided feature importance values which allowed the statistical features used for classification to be compared. The CNN models used in this work do not provide the same direct interpretation of which parts of the light curve were important for each prediction. Future work could therefore focus on interpreting the CNN predictions to determine whether features such as depth, duration, shape, or repeated events are driving the classifications. This would also help determine whether the CNN is learning information beyond the statistical features provided to the random forest models.

Future analysis could also compare the individual misclassifications from the random forest and CNN models. Although the models achieved similar overall evaluation metrics, this does not show whether the same light curves were classified incorrectly by both models. Comparing the overlap between their misclassifications could show whether both algorithms struggle with the same types of light curves or whether each model gains useful information from different features. If there is a difference in the misclassifications between the two types of models, this could lead to an expanded model which combines the full light-curve input of the CNN with extracted features similar to those used by the random forest.

CNN models can also be injected with extra information. \textbf{(Also get those papers about injecting things like radius ratio and impact parameter)} shows the application of physical parameters such as flux ratio and impact parameter. They use several convolution layers in different set ups to the architecture used in this paper, but provide feature values to the network before proceeding to the dense layer. The random forest results in this paper have shown the importance of extracted features compared to the entire data set. While this paper is focused on using minimal data processing, finding a balance with feature injection could improve future models. 

There are a few things which could be done to the models in a future project to improve results. The model layers can be altered to include more convolutions or dense learning layers. More convolution would help to create larger feature maps for the model to compare from. As well altering kernel sizes to fit more data in the filter could be helpful. The use of dropout to limit overfitting appears to have worked correctly and thus adding more overfitting prevention steps likely will not improve results. As the data is relatively simple for the CNN, massively increasing the number of layers is unlikely to provide noticeable improvements. The model used TensorFlow Keras layers which work very well but the library also allows for custom layers to be developed. Utilizing this, it may be possible to make a convolution layer customized for the specific case of light curve classification. 

Models can also be trained using other models and this method is utilized in many machine learning cases across different fields. One way to use this in this work could be to develop synthetic data to initially train the model off of. If the generated data is made to be statistically significant compared to the real Kepler data, it can be used to make a basic starting model. Then, by creating a strong model off the synthetic light curves, the architecture can be reused on real data to potentially struggle less with the classification of real light curves.  

Other missions can help expand future models performance in testing if the limited preprocessing approach is applicable in more cases. The models were created, trained, and evaluated based on the Kepler mission which is a very good base to work off of as it is very well documented. This same performance cannot be assumed to repeat in other transit detection missions. Applying the same process to the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) would be very useful in determining whether the approach in this paper is more generalizable or limited to the Kepler mission. It is not expected that the exact same models developed in this paper would perform equally well with TESS or other missions, as the observations can differ in properties such as cadence, noise characteristics, observational baseline, and instrumental effects.

\subsection{Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS)} \label{sec:TESS}

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/images/TESS_vs_Kepler}
    \caption{Comparison of the survey strategies of the \textit{Kepler} and TESS missions\protect\footnotemark[4]{}. \textit{Kepler} focused on a deep search of a small region of the sky, while TESS conducts a wide-field survey targeting nearby stars across nearly the entire sky.}
    \label{fig:TESS_vs_Kepler}
\end{figure}

\footnotetext[4]{\url{https://www.nasa.gov/space-science-and-astrobiology-at-ames/division-overview/astrophysics-branch-overview-sta/exoplanet-mission/exoplanet-research-projects}}

Following the success of the \textit{Kepler} and K2 missions, NASA launched the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) to carry out a wide-field survey of nearly the entire sky, focusing on nearby, bright stars well suited for follow-up characterization \citep{Ricker2015, Huang2018}. This observing strategy puts emphasis on short-period planets orbiting luminous host stars which are better suited for precision mass measurements and atmospheric studies.

TESS monitors the sky in sectors with typical observing baselines of $\sim27$ days each. Compared to \textit{Kepler}, which maintained continuous observations of the same field, this is significantly shorter. Figure~\ref{fig:TESS_vs_Kepler} shows an artist rendition of the field of view covered by TESS in comparison to that of \textit{Kepler}. As a result, TESS is most sensitive to planets with orbital periods shorter than $\sim10$–$20$ days. Longer period planets remain better constrained by the extended \textit{Kepler} dataset \citep{Sullivan2015}. Despite this limitation, TESS has greatly expanded the known population of transiting exoplanets and produced thousands of candidates orbiting bright nearby stars across the sky \citep{Guerrero2021}.

Together, \textit{Kepler} and TESS complement one another on insights into exoplanet populations. The long baseline and high photometric precision of \textit{Kepler} allowed for statistical studies of population occurrence rates and structure. One prominent result is the discovery of the “radius valley”, a deficit of planets with radii around $1.5$–$2R_{\oplus}$ that separates two dominant populations of super-Earths and sub-Neptunes \citep{2017AJ....154..109F, 2018MNRAS.479.4786V}. In contrast, TESS primarily detects planets orbiting bright nearby stars, making these systems well suited for detailed follow-up observations. These include radial velocity mass measurements and atmospheric characterization with facilities such as the James Webb Space Telescope. TESS is the connection between the statistical studies of exoplanet populations and parameter estimation of planetary systems \citep{Batalha2019}.

\subsection{PLAnetary Transits and Oscillations of stars (PLATO)} \label{sec:PLATO}

Looking ahead, the European Space Agency (ESA) is preparing the next major mission in exoplanet transit research. Known as the \textit{PLAnetary Transits and Oscillations of stars} (PLATO) mission, its objective is to extend the work \textit{Kepler} and TESS by introducing high-precision, long-baseline photometric monitoring of bright stars across large areas of the sky \citep{Rauer2014}. The mission is currently scheduled to launch between late 2026 and early 2027. PLATO's primary goal is to detect and characterize terrestrial exoplanets orbiting within the habitable zones of their host stars. Furthermore, the mission will perform asteroseisomology to determine precise stellar ages, radii, and masses.

PLATO will combine wide-field coverage with long-duration observations lasting several years in selected fields. In contrast to TESS, PLATO's strategy is optimized for detecting small planets with longer orbital periods which are much more difficult to observe with current all-sky transit surveys \citep{Rauer2014}. The mission is expected to produce a statistically robust sample of Earth-sized planets with well constrained host-star properties. Such measurements will enable detailed study of planetary system evolution and improve estimates of habitable worlds in the solar neighbourhood. 

Together, PLATO, \textit{Kepler}, and TESS form a complementary sequence of transit surveys. Collectively, they span deep narrow-field observations, all-sky coverage of bright stars, and future baseline characterization of nearby terrestrial systems.  Within this progression, PLATO is anticipated to provide the precise stellar and planetary parameters necessary to connect statistical exoplanet demographics with detailed physical characterization \citep{PLATO2022}.
